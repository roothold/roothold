The anchor economy

Durable assets, a compounding network, and why belonging is a moat.

FirstLight EditorialJuly 30, 20266 min read

Capital is looking for something it hasn't had much of lately: assets that are genuinely hard to reproduce, in markets that are genuinely growing, that throw off revenue which compounds instead of churns. The anchor economy is built to be exactly that.

Start with the real estate. Foreign-Trade Zone campuses with on-site manufacturing and shared cold storage sit in one of the most supply-constrained corners of the industrial market. Cold storage in particular is expensive to build, power-hungry, and chronically undersupplied — which is precisely why it commands premium rents and sticky, long-duration tenants.

A community you belong to beats a place you pass through — and that retention compounds.
The Moat

The market behind the asset

RootHold's campuses are pointed at four of the fastest-growing, most cold-chain-dependent categories: food and beverage, health, beauty, and pharmaceuticals. These are not speculative bets — they are large markets compounding at high single- to double-digit rates, with the temperature-controlled slices growing fastest of all.

The compounding network

On top of durable real estate sits something rarer: a membership platform. RootHold's operating companies grow from one hundred toward three hundred businesses at a million dollars or more in annual recurring revenue. Each new company deepens the network, and the network makes each company harder to leave. That's the difference between a lease and a moat.

Why now, why us

Three things rarely line up: durable real assets, a network that compounds, and measurable impact — jobs, tax base, and regions rebuilt around something that lasts. The anchor economy offers all three at once. For partners backing what endures, that is the whole thesis.
