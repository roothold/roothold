Why cities compete to be chosen

For a century, cities chased companies. The best ones are learning to be chased.

FirstLight EditorialJuly 16, 20265 min read

The old playbook was simple and expensive: a city wanted growth, so it wrote a check. Bigger incentives, bigger buildings, bigger promises. The problem was never the ambition. It was that the thing being courted could always leave — and often did, taking the jobs and leaving the tax abatement behind.

A different kind of project changes that math. When what a region attracts is an anchor — a campus other companies organize themselves around — the leverage inverts. The anchor doesn't need any one city. Cities need the anchor. And so, for the first time in a long time, cities compete to be chosen.

The anchor economy rewards the cities bold enough to be chosen — not the ones willing to pay the most.

What a city brings

A RootHold campus is a partnership, not a subsidy. The city brings what only it can: developable land, a Foreign-Trade Zone footprint, workforce and training pipelines, port and rail access, and an economic-development team that can move. RootHold brings the anchor and the operating companies that make it real — and stays, because belonging is the business model.

The multiplier a city keeps

The return isn't a single tenant. It's an ecosystem: an investment on the order of a quarter-billion dollars, thousands of direct jobs, a new tax base, rising property values, and a roster of co-located companies and suppliers that no one building could ever create. Acres become an economy — value that compounds locally, year after year.
